How I Stopped Scheduling My Time And Started Scheduling My Energy

By Theo Marsh · 11 May 2027

I used to build my whole day around a clock. Wake at a set time, work block at a set time, gym at a set time, lights out at a set time. It looked disciplined from the outside. From the inside it felt like dragging a body that had nothing left through a schedule that didn't care.

That was the burnout job, before the retreat center, before any of this. I had the calendar of someone thriving and the energy of someone running on fumes, and for a long time I thought the fix was more willpower. Push harder through the slump. Grind past the wall. Everyone around me called it discipline.

It wasn't discipline. It was just spending down a battery I never let recharge.

Years living inside a monastic schedule undid a lot of that thinking, mostly by accident. Nobody at the retreat center talked about energy management. They just built a day around fixed points, sit here, walk there, eat then, sleep then, and something in my body relaxed in a way no amount of scheduling my time ever managed. I didn't understand why until much later, after I'd left, after I'd started logging my own days in the same paper journal I still keep.

What the years of pages told me, eventually, is that my best days were never the ones where I forced the most out of myself. They were the ones where the first few hours ran on something close to autopilot, so the energy I actually had left could go toward the one or two things that mattered.

So no, this isn't another piece about finding more hours in the day. There aren't more hours. There's only what you have the energy to actually do with the ones you get.

What's The Difference Between A Routine And A Ritual?

1. A routine gets something done.

A routine is the plain version. Meal prep, a calendar block, the gym at the same hour every day. Useful, but if you run it without attention it turns robotic, and robotic things are the first to get skipped the moment life gets loud.

2. A ritual signals something to your body.

Same action, different outcome. A ritual is the same behavior done with a little intention and a sensory cue attached to it, and that small addition is what makes your nervous system treat it as safe rather than optional. Predictability calms the body. Heart rate steadies, cortisol comes down, and the whole system stops bracing.

There's a decent explanation for why this works underneath the hood. Willpower and discipline run through the prefrontal cortex, and that part of the brain is expensive to run. It burns through metabolic energy fast. A ritual, once it's actually a ritual and not just a plan you keep renegotiating, moves out of that expensive real estate and into the basal ganglia instead, the part of the brain that runs on autopilot the same way driving home does. Low energy cost, self sustaining. You stop spending willpower on it because you're no longer deciding. You're just doing the thing you already decided, days or years ago.

The energy trap

Energy, not time, is what productivity actually runs on. You can have every hour of the day free and still get nothing done in it if you're depleted. And most of us do the opposite of what would fix that. We're overworked mentally and underworked physically, sitting long hours and sleeping and moving too little, and as our capacity falls we respond by trying to work even harder to close the gap. That just digs the hole deeper.

The days that actually feel good, the ones where the work has a kind of lightness to it, aren't the days I pushed hardest. They're the days I'd already spent almost no energy on the small stuff, because the small stuff ran on rituals instead of decisions.

My Own Rituals

Morning

Movement before input. Body first, screen second. My sacral energy, or whatever you want to call the raw physical charge you wake up with, needs to move before it gets asked to think. So the sequence never changes: out of bed, straight outside, a short walk before I look at a single message.

After that, water and something that actually holds me, not a quick sugar hit that crashes an hour later. Then I set one line of intention for the day, not a to do list, just a sense of what I actually want the hours to add up to. That's the whole morning ritual. Under five minutes of actual attention, though the walk runs longer.

Midday

I move my body for a few minutes roughly once an hour. Nothing dramatic. Stand, stretch, walk to the kitchen and back. The point isn't exercise, it's refusing to let the body go completely still for six hours straight.

When I get stuck, I switch gears entirely rather than grinding at the same wall. Grab a blank page and sketch the problem out by hand instead of typing it. Walk and think out loud instead of sitting and staring. Getting stuck drains energy fast, so the fix is changing the mode, not pushing harder in the same one.

And when the afternoon dip hits, which it always does, I let it. A short rest, sometimes an actual nap that I end the second the alarm goes, because a nap that runs long just trades one kind of tired for another.

Evening

I close the loop on the day before I try to close the day itself. Finish what can be finished, write down what can't be, so the thoughts don't spend all evening looping in the back of my head uninvited.

Then a buffer. Something that marks the switch from work mode to the rest of my life, even if it's only a few minutes of nothing in particular. Skip that step and the stress just follows me into the house.

The wind down itself never changes shape. Phone down, lights low, the same few motions every night, because the sequence itself is what tells my brain it's time to power down, not any single ingredient in it. Before bed I also try to physically discharge whatever the day left in my body, some stretching, a short walk, so I'm not lying there still buzzing from six hours ago.

  • Phone down
  • Lights dimmed
  • Same few minutes of stretching
  • Same short window of quiet before the light goes off

What I've Learned After Years Of Building These

3. Keep it under a few minutes or it won't survive a bad week.

Every ritual I've ever abandoned was too long to begin with. The ones that lasted years all started small enough that skipping felt more effortful than doing them.

4. The point is not to optimize it.

Test something for a while, notice if it's actually easier to start, and if the answer is yes, stop fiddling with it. I've watched myself ruin a perfectly good ritual by trying to improve it every few weeks. Improvement is a different project than consistency. Don't run both at once.

One ritual, not five habits

I get the pull to overhaul everything at once. I've done it, more than once, and every time the whole structure collapsed within a month. One small ritual, repeated at the same trigger every time, will carry you through weeks where motivation has completely left the building. Five new habits started on the same Monday will not.

Someone I used to study under put it more plainly than I ever could: a ritual is a decision you've already made. I'd add only this. Once it's made, stop remaking it every single day. That's the whole relief of the thing.

Common Questions About Building Rituals Around Energy

What if I miss a day?

You resume the next one. That's it. The habit journal on my shelf has gaps in it going back years, and none of those gaps mattered nearly as much as I thought they would at the time. A ritual is about the relationship you keep with it over years, not a perfect streak.

How long should a ritual take before it counts as one?

There's no minimum. Some of mine run two minutes. What makes it a ritual isn't the length, it's that the same cue leads to the same small action without a debate happening first.

Do I need discipline to start?

No. You need one cue and one action small enough that skipping it takes more thought than doing it. Discipline is what people call it when they haven't built the ritual yet and are still white knuckling their way through the decision every single day. Build the ritual and the discipline question mostly stops coming up.
