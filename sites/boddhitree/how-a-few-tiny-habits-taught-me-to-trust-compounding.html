How A Few Tiny Habits Taught Me To Trust Compounding

By Theo Marsh · 5 December 2026

Something changed in my mornings a few years ago, and it did not come from anything dramatic. I did not quit a job or move back to a retreat center. I put my phone across the room before I went to sleep so I could not reach it when the alarm went off. That was the whole intervention.

I want to tell you it felt significant at the time. It did not. It felt almost embarrassing, actually, to admit that the thing standing between me and a calmer morning was where I set my phone down the night before. But I logged it anyway, in the same paper journal I have kept for decades, and a week later I noticed I had not hit snooze once.

That is the part nobody tells you about small habits. They do not announce themselves. There is no ceremony. You wake up one Tuesday and realize something about your day is different, and it takes you a minute to trace it back to the tiny, almost silly thing you started three weeks earlier.

Before I recommend anything to a reader, I test it on myself for at least ninety days. Most habits fail that test. The ones that survive are almost never the ambitious overhauls I used to attempt, back when I thought change had to be big to count.

You have probably tried the big version too. New Year's resolution, complete diet overhaul, an hour of exercise starting Monday. Did it stick?

No. Mine never did either. Ninety two percent of resolutions made in January fail, and I do not think that number is about willpower. I think it is about scale.

What actually works, in my own life and in every page of that journal, is absurdly small. So small it barely counts as a change at all. That is the part worth explaining properly.

What A Micro Habit Actually Is

The size that survives a bad day

A micro habit is a version of a habit small enough to do on the worst day you have had all month, not the healthy, motivated version of yourself. If a habit only survives on your best days, it is not a habit yet. It is a hope.

Every habit, big or small, runs on the same loop of cue, routine, and reward. Brewing the coffee is the cue. Sitting down to write a page in my journal is the routine. Enjoying the coffee while I do it is the reward. Once that loop repeats enough times, the behavior stops needing a decision from you at all. It just runs.

Something like 95 percent of what we do in a day runs on this kind of autopilot, not on conscious choice. That number used to surprise me. It does not anymore. I watch it happen in my own house every morning.

Why the tiny version wins

Habits behave more like interest in a savings account than like a single decision. One pushup, one sentence, one page does not look like much today. But improve by just 1 percent a day, and by the end of a year you are not 1 percent better. You are roughly 37 times better, because the gains compound on top of each other instead of adding up in a straight line.

I think about this every time I flip back through old pages in my journal. The habits that actually reshaped my life were never the loud ones. They were the ones so small I almost did not bother writing them down.

What I Actually Kept

The honest list

Here it is, pulled straight from years of my own notes, not the version that sounds impressive.

  • Phone charging across the room, out of reach of the bed
  • A glass of water every time I make coffee, since I already make coffee without thinking about it
  • Ten to fifteen minutes of journaling before I do anything else
  • A book beside the bed and ten minutes of reading before sleep instead of a screen
  • My bedtime moved fifteen minutes earlier, held there for two months, then moved another fifteen
  • Gym clothes laid out the night before, socks included
  • The kitchen sink left clean before I go to bed

None of these took willpower. That is the point. I attached each one to something I was already doing without thinking, so the new habit had somewhere to hold on instead of floating on its own, waiting for motivation that was not reliably going to show up.

1. Attach it to something that already happens without thinking

If you already brush your teeth every night without fail, that is your anchor. Meditate right after. If you already make coffee, drink the water while it brews. The existing habit becomes the cue for the new one, and you stop needing to remember it on purpose.

2. Write it down before you do it

You are roughly 42 percent more likely to actually do something once you have written it down. I write the next day's short list the night before, coffee still in hand from journaling. It is a small habit that ends up protecting several other small habits.

What My Own Tracking Taught Me

1. A missed day does not undo the habit

I used to think one skipped morning meant starting over from zero. It does not. Habits take somewhere between 18 and 254 days to form, depending on the habit and the person, and one missed rep inside that window barely registers. What actually breaks a habit is deciding, after missing once, that you have already failed and there is no point continuing.

2. Progress beats perfection, every time I have tracked it

I do not improve 1 percent every single day. Nobody honestly does. What I have watched work, in my own log, is improving most days. That is enough. That is more than enough, actually.

Move the goalpost by minutes, not miles

My bedtime did not move a full hour. It moved fifteen minutes, held there until it stopped feeling like an effort, then moved another fifteen. Trying to gain a full hour of sleep in one attempt would have failed the same week I started, the way it always had before.

Questions I Still Get About This

How small is too small to count?

It is not. One pushup counts. One sentence counts. If logging it feels almost silly, that is usually a sign you have found the right size for it.

What if I want to add several habits at once?

Add them one at a time and let the first one settle before you stack a second on top. I learned this one the hard way, by trying to change four parts of my day in the same week and abandoning all four inside ten days.

How long until it feels automatic?

Long enough that you will stop noticing you are doing it before you notice how much it has changed. Mine took closer to two months than two weeks. Yours might take longer. Keep the log anyway. You will want the proof later, even if you do not need it right now.
