What Finally Taught My Evenings To Slow All The Way Down

By Theo Marsh · 9 September 2026

My evenings used to be nothing but catch up mode. Answering messages, squeezing in one more task, cleaning the kitchen while my head was already rehearsing tomorrow. Then I would finally get into bed and my mind kept going everywhere.

True rest, I have learned after years of testing this on myself, starts long before your head ever touches the pillow. It starts with how you wind down.

I used to think the real work of sleep happened inside the bedroom itself, in the mattress and the blackout blinds and the white noise. Most of that work actually happens in the hour and a half before you ever walk through that door.

The body does not drop into sleep because you tell it to relax. It drops into sleep because the environment and the behavior around it finally stop sending the message that there is still something left to handle.

I have spent years building this evening sequence, one piece at a time, keeping each addition to myself for months before I ever mentioned it to anyone else. Some pieces stuck. Some did not survive a single week.

What is left now is a handoff, not a list of separate tasks bolted together. Each part quiets the body a little more before passing it to the next, the way one section of an orchestra has to come down before another can rise.

This is the version I return to, night after night. Take what serves you, and leave the rest.

What Is Actually Changing When You Wind Down

More than a calm mind

For a long time I built my evenings around one goal, settling my mind. Reading, stretching, dim lighting, all of it helps, but none of it automatically fixes a body clock that has drifted, a stress response that is still revved up, or breathing that never gets the chance to slow down. Sleep depends on timing, on breathing mechanics, on circulation, and on the state of the nervous system, not just on how calm your thoughts feel by the time you lie down.

The body trusts rhythm more than good intentions

Researchers who tracked nearly two thousand older adults through Duke Health found that people with irregular sleep patterns carried higher body weight, higher blood sugar, higher blood pressure, and a greater ten year risk of heart attack or stroke than people who kept regular bedtimes and wake times. Your body does not experience sleep as something you can make up for with one good routine before bed. It responds to repetition. If your evening changes shape every night, your body has to guess when sleep is actually coming.

Two systems that have to change gears

You do not fall asleep well in a body that is still acting like it needs to solve problems. One branch of the nervous system handles mobilization. The other supports rest, digestion, and the drop in physical drive that sleep actually requires. That shift never happens because you decide to relax. It happens because your environment and your behavior finally stop sending the signal to stay switched on.

My Own Wind Down, Start To Finish

The phone goes first

Around the same time every evening, my phone goes onto do not disturb and leaves the room entirely. We do not keep phones in the bedroom at all anymore, and I have absolutely come to love that rule. It removes so much of the temptation to keep scrolling late at night, and it gives my brain one clear signal, it is safe to switch off now.

Tea, then a hot shower or bath

I brew something herbal, usually chamomile, peppermint, or a blend with a little valerian root and passionflower. The simplicity of it is the whole point. As soon as I breathe in the steam and wrap my hands around a hot mug, my shoulders start to soften on their own. Some nights that is a bath, magnesium salt included for the body's salt balance, with a drop or two of an essential oil blend meant for letting go. Other nights it is only a shower, quicker and plainer, with lavender or chamomile in the steam. Either one resets me from the day and brings me back into my body before I ever reach the bedroom.

A few minutes for the body

I keep a balm on my nightstand for a short self massage before my head hits the pillow, working from the temples along the jaw and down the neck, then the hands, then finally the feet. It is a few minutes of softening skin and releasing whatever tension the day left behind. On nights I do a proper skin ritual instead, it is never really about the products. It is about presence, a gentle cleanse followed by something nourishing, a way of physically releasing the day rather than just thinking my way out of it.

Emptying my head onto paper

This is where I clear space, mentally and emotionally, before I ever try to sleep. I ask myself what is still lingering, what felt hard today, and what I need to release so I am not carrying it into tomorrow. Sometimes a limiting belief surfaces, something like "I did not do enough today," and when it does I find real evidence against it instead of letting it sit there unanswered. A few lines of gratitude go in too, whatever is actually true that day, not a performance of positivity.

Breath as the last step

Before I try to sleep I sit for a few minutes of meditation, sometimes guided, sometimes just quiet breathing on my own. My favorite pattern is a longer exhale than inhale, in for a count of four, out for a count of six, repeated for a few minutes until my mind stops chasing itself. Once I am actually in bed, I do not chase sleep. I let the inhale stay soft through the nose and let the exhale do the work of lowering the effort, relaxing my jaw, my tongue, my forehead, and if a thought arrives I just come back to the next exhale.

What Years Of This Have Actually Taught Me

Consistency beats an elaborate ritual

Keeping it simple and doing it every night matters more than how impressive any single evening looks. Starting to wind down before your body forces you to, even for fifteen or twenty minutes of the same simple things each night, trains your body to rest better over time. It adds up in a way that one perfect, complicated evening never will.

Having a bedtime routine and having an effective one are not the same thing. Most parents use some kind of bedtime routine with their kids, yet only about one in five adults with a routine of their own say it actually helps them sleep significantly better. A routine can exist for years without producing much of a result if it starts too late, if the steps stay passive instead of doing anything for the body, or if the order changes so often that the brain never learns to link it with sleep.

Missed nights do not erase progress

People tend to think consistency means never missing a night. It does not. It means returning quickly instead of never missing. Structured bedtime routines tend to show their most noticeable improvement within the first three nights, with the rest continuing to build over the following two weeks as the pattern settles in. If travel or stress or a late shift knocks a night out, the answer is not to quit for a week. It is to pick the smallest version of the ritual back up at the next opportunity.

You do not need all of it

You by no means need every piece of this every night. Life happens, and it is unlikely that any evening will look exactly the same twice. Some nights the ritual is five minutes long, washing my face while feeling genuinely grateful that the day is done. It still counts. Give yourself permission to let go of what you think you should be doing and just do what actually serves you that particular night.

The Questions I Get Asked Most

What if I do not have time for all of this?

Then you do not do all of it. Five minutes is still a ritual if it is repeated with intention. The version that fits your actual evening beats the version you saw someone else post about.

  • Washing your face while you feel genuinely grateful the day is over
  • Brewing one cup of tea and drinking it slowly, with nothing else happening
  • Sitting quietly for a minute with one hand on your chest and one on your belly

What if I still cannot fall asleep?

Do not add more effort. Reduce it. Stay off the clock, keep the lights low, and if you need to get out of bed, do one calm activity away from any screen until your body feels sleepy again. Bed should feel like the place sleep happens, not the place you wrestle with it.

What about waking up in the middle of the night?

Keep the response boring on purpose. No bright lights, no scrolling, no checking anything work related. Use a calm, familiar reset, slow breathing through the nose or a few minutes of reading in low light, and go back to bed once your body actually feels sleepy again. The goal is never to hack your way back to sleep. It is to avoid teaching your brain that waking in the night is a reason to become fully alert.
