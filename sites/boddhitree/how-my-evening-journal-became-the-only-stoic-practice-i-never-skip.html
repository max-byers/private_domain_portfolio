How My Evening Journal Became The Only Stoic Practice I Never Skip

By Theo Marsh · 17 March 2027

I have been keeping a paper journal for decades now, but for a long stretch of those years it had nothing to do with Stoicism. It was where I logged habits and crossed off boxes. The Stoic part came later, once I understood that for Marcus Aurelius, Seneca, and Epictetus, the notebook was never separate from the philosophy. It was the philosophy.

I trained under a few different teachers across Buddhist and Stoic traditions over the years, and I never expected the plainest tool in either toolbox to be a pen and a blank page. But it is. Marcus Aurelius wrote what became Meditations without ever expecting an audience. He titled it, in the original Greek, Ta eis heauton. To himself. It was never meant for anyone else's eyes, and that is exactly why it still works.

Seneca kept the habit too, at night, after the house had gone quiet. Epictetus told his students to write down their philosophy day by day, to read it aloud, to talk to themselves and each other about it. Three very different lives. One shared habit.

What follows is not a theory of why Stoic journaling works. It is what I actually do, most mornings and most nights, and what nearly two decades of doing it has taught me about the difference between journaling and Stoic journaling.

What Stoic Journaling Actually Is

Three men, one habit

Epictetus was born a slave. Marcus Aurelius was an emperor. Seneca was a power broker and a playwright. Their lives had almost nothing in common, and yet each of them kept the same practice. Epictetus admonished his students that philosophy was something they should write down day by day, that the writing itself was how they were meant to exercise themselves. Seneca preferred the evenings. Once the light had been taken away and his wife had fallen silent, he would examine his entire day and go back over what he had done and said, hiding nothing from himself, passing nothing by. He said the sleep that followed that self examination was particularly sweet.

Marcus was the most prodigious journaler of the three, and we are fortunate that his notebook survived at all. The philosopher Brand Blanshard once marveled that few people now care about the marches and countermarches of the Roman commanders, but the centuries have clung to a notebook of thoughts by a man whose real life was largely unknown, written in the midnight dimness, holding not the events of the day but something of far more permanent interest, the ideals a rare spirit lived by.

Not a diary

The historian Michel Foucault noticed the same habit running through this entire period of history, a culture of what he called personal writing: taking notes on the reading, conversations, and reflections one hears or engages in, keeping notebooks the Greeks called hupomnemata, reread from time to time so their contents could be lived again.

A Stoic journal is not a diary in the ordinary sense. It is not a record of what happened to you. It is where you prepare for the day ahead, reflect on the day that passed, and remind yourself of what you have already learned from your teachers, your reading, and your own experience, over and over, until the lesson stops being something you once heard and becomes something you actually do. This daily practice is the philosophy, not a supplement to it. It is nearly impossible to have one without the other.

My Own Practice, Morning To Night

The morning page

Marcus was an early riser. He reminded himself every morning not to waste the time he had been granted by huddling under the blankets, and he used the first minutes of the day to meditate on the difficulties that lay ahead so he would meet them already prepared. Seneca agreed. He told his friend Lucilius that we are more industrious when we anticipate the day and welcome the dawn, and that we let ourselves down when we lie dozing while the sun climbs the sky.

My own morning page is short. I ask myself what is actually up to me in whatever the day's biggest event happens to be, and what is not. I name one thing I am choosing to practice, patience most days, and I think through what might test it before it actually does. Marcus did something close to this himself, telling himself each morning that the people he would deal with that day would be meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, and surly, not to sour his mood before it started, but so that none of it could catch him unprepared.

The evening review

The evening is where I spend more of my page. Seneca's version of this ritual has outlived him by two thousand years for good reason. When the light had been removed and his wife had fallen silent, aware of the habit that was now his, he examined his entire day and went back over what he had done and said, hiding nothing from himself, passing nothing by.

Both Seneca and Epictetus point back to an older source for this practice, the Golden Verses of Pythagoras, and the questions in it are still the ones I ask myself most nights. What did you do amiss? What did you do? What duty was left undone? Epictetus put the same instinct into verse of his own, admitting not sleep into your tender eyelids until you have reckoned up each deed of the day, how you erred, what you did or left undone. For good deeds, be glad. For poor ones, correct course tomorrow rather than punish yourself tonight.

I keep three plain prompts most nights, close to the ones I first found written about this practice:

  • What did I do well today?
  • What could I have done better today?
  • How will I improve tomorrow?

The goal is never to make myself feel small over the mistakes. It is only to notice them clearly enough that I do not repeat them.

The last few lines

Before I put the notebook down, I do one more short thing, something close to what Seneca called a contemplation of finality. As he put it, one who daily puts the finishing touches on his life is never short of time. I write a version of his own closing line to Lucilius. I have lived. The day that was given to me is finished. And if I am given another one tomorrow, I mean to receive it gladly rather than owe it anything.

What Years Of This Have Actually Taught Me

Frequency beats intensity

I have tried the long, carefully written entry and I have tried the one honest sentence scratched down half asleep. The one sentence wins nearly every time, not because it is better writing, but because it is the version that actually survives a bad week. Three to ten minutes, morning and night combined, has changed more for me than an hour once a month ever did. Consistency has always mattered more than how impressive any single entry looks on the page.

It was never about the prose

Tim Ferriss, who journals every morning himself, has said he does not do it to be productive, or to find great ideas, or to put down anything he could later publish. The pages are not for anyone but him. He is caging his own monkey mind on paper so he can get on with his day. I understand that completely. My own pages are not well written. They were never supposed to be. Stoic journaling is not about beautiful prose. It is about practicing a beautiful life, one honest, unglamorous entry at a time.

The point is action, not thinking

Stoicism was never only about thinking well. It is about acting well, and the only honest way I have found to improve at that is to review the actions afterward. Did I follow through on what I planned for the day. Was I actually prepared. What could I have done better. What did I learn today that will help me tomorrow. Those four questions, asked in that order most evenings, have taught me more about my own patterns than any single thing I read on the subject ever did.

Questions I Get Asked Most

What if I miss a day?

Then you missed a day. Missed a day, not missed the practice for good. Perfectionism is the fastest way to kill this habit before it ever becomes one. If you skip a night, pick it back up the next one. Frequency beats intensity here every single time, and one missed page has never undone the ones that came before it.

What if I do not know what to write?

Start smaller than feels reasonable. One sentence still counts. Pick a stable format and keep asking the same handful of questions each time so you are not also deciding what to write about on top of actually writing it. A problem well stated is already halfway solved, and most nights that means naming exactly what happened rather than reaching for something profound.

Do I need to actually believe in Stoicism for this to work?

No. What you need is a page, a pen, and a stable time you actually return to. Whatever form is most workable for you is the right one. Some people prefer a notebook. Some prefer an empty document on a screen. What matters is the process, not the format, and the value of it compounds the same way any real habit does, little by little, practice by practice, for as long as you keep showing up.
