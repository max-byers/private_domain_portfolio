The Saturday Ritual That Keeps Me From Drifting Off Course

By Theo Marsh · 11 December 2027

I have not missed a Saturday morning check in for over a year now. The year before that I missed maybe six weeks, and I remember every one of them because I felt it by the following Tuesday. Nothing dramatic happens when I skip it. I just drift a little further off course than I notice at the time.

When I first came back from living at the retreat center, I assumed the discipline would carry over into ordinary life on its own. It did not. Within a few months I was working sixty hours a week again and telling myself I would rest once things settled down. Things never settled down.

The problem was never that I lacked willpower. The problem was that I never stopped long enough to ask myself the right questions. My mistakes were not some deep mystery I needed years of therapy to uncover. Most of them were obvious, the moment I actually looked for them.

That is the whole case for what I am about to describe. Reflecting on your week, on purpose, on a schedule, so that looking becomes the default instead of something you only do once a crisis forces your hand.

I tested versions of this for well over ninety days before I trusted any of it enough to write about it, which is how I test everything now. Some pieces I kept. Others I threw out within a month because they made the ritual feel like homework instead of a room I actually wanted to walk into.

What follows is the version that stuck.

Why Stop To Look At All?

The wasted motion problem

A lot of what goes wrong in an ordinary week is not some hidden flaw in your character. It is just wasted motion, actions that quietly fail to get you where you meant to go. Most weeks that goes completely unnoticed unless someone points it out to you, or unless you build in a regular moment to look for it yourself. A few questions I come back to most:

  • What mistakes did I make this week?
  • What am I missing?
  • What was my biggest success this week?
  • What is my current biggest priority?
  • What is the right lesson to take from this week?

Turning something vague into something you can actually answer

It is far easier to explain something after it has already happened than to predict it in advance. The past feels concrete. The future feels vague, so your mind slides right past it. You can force that same clarity backward, on purpose, before the week is even over. I find it almost impossible to catch my own mistakes as I am making them, but if you ask me directly what the biggest mistake of my week was, I can usually answer within a minute. The trick is making not answering the question something you do not let yourself get away with.

What My Saturday Actually Looks Like

I do mine every Saturday morning, same time, same chair, a cup of tea before anyone else in the house is awake. The time itself barely matters. What matters is that it never moves, so there is never a decision about whether to do it, only about what I find once I get there.

1. Closing out what already happened

Before I plan anything new, I close out the week that just ended. I pull out my paper habit tracker (I have kept one for years, never an app) and fill in whatever I forgot to mark during the week itself, because if I do not do it now I will never remember it later. I look at what I spent money on and where I lost time, and I sort each loss into a rough category: whether it was avoidance, an impulse, overcommitment, or plain disorganization. I am not diagnosing my character. I am just finding the leak.

2. Finding the actual root of it

For each thing that went wrong, I ask myself one question: what was I actually trying to get, avoid, or soothe? That question does more work than any amount of willpower ever has. If I catch myself scrolling my phone late at night again, is it really boredom? No. It is usually that I am avoiding how uncomfortable tomorrow's first task is going to feel.

Naming what actually went right

I also write down three wins, even during a rough week, because they show me what is actually working and worth repeating. Then I go through my calendar for everyone I talked to that week and think about who I feel grateful for in hindsight. If the feeling is real, not forced, I write them a short note about the specific thing I valued and send it. This is, far and away the most fun part of the ritual, and it happens to be the part most likely to make the whole habit stick on the weeks when the rest of it feels like a chore.

One thing, not ten

Once I can see the week clearly, I pick exactly one change for the week ahead. Not ten. Not even three. One. It might be as small as packing my gym bag the night before, or moving my phone out of the bedroom. Trying to fix everything at once is how I used to talk myself out of fixing anything, so now I deliberately keep it to a single, specific move.

What Changes After A Few Months

For the first few weeks, honestly, not much happens. Some Saturdays turn up nothing interesting at all, and I have learned to leave those alone rather than force an insight that is not there. What you are actually doing in those early weeks is building up enough entries to eventually see across them. Give it at least a month before you decide whether any of this is working for you.

Then, without really planning to, you start noticing patterns. You see which habits actually helped you and which ones quietly drained you. You catch the same excuse showing up in three different weeks and finally recognize it as an excuse instead of a fact. I once heard someone describe the weekly review as preventative maintenance for the brain, the same way a car eventually breaks down if nobody services it regularly, and I think that is exactly right. Most of us take far better care of our cars than we do our own minds.

None of this requires a perfect week. It never has, for me. The goal was never a flawless streak. It is a week that is a little better than the one before it, and that is a much lower bar to clear than most people assume going in.

Doing it with someone else

I also check in with a friend most weeks, a short call where we each say what our one thing was and whether we actually did it. Saying it out loud to another person makes it real in a way that only telling myself never quite managed. You do not need a whole group for this. One person who will actually ask you next week is enough.

What If I Miss A Week?

You will, eventually. I have missed weeks, and the routine survived every one of them. What matters is not turning one missed week into an excuse to quit the whole thing. A good version of this ritual is flexible enough to absorb a few missed weeks here and there. You keep doing it because it is useful, not because you are protecting some perfect streak.

How long before I know if this is actually worth my time?

Give it three weeks before you decide anything. Treat it as an experiment rather than a lifetime commitment. That alone removes most of the resistance for me. At the end of three weeks, ask honestly whether it helped. If it did, keep going and let it compound from there. If it did not, you have lost very little, and you can just let it go.
