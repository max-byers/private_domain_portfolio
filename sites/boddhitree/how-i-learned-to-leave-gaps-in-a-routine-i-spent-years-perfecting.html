How I Learned To Leave Gaps In A Routine I Spent Years Perfecting

By Theo Marsh · 20 September 2027

For years I treated an open hour on my calendar as a problem to solve. If a Tuesday afternoon had nothing written in it, I filled it. Another task, another errand, another small ritual borrowed from a book I liked. My paper journal is full of these years, page after page of hours accounted for, and for a long time I read that as proof I was doing this well.

Then I went on a short trip and came home wanting to throw the whole system out.

Nothing dramatic happened on that trip. I just stopped checking my plan for four days, ate what was in front of me, talked with people I loved for longer than usual, and did not once look at the sequence I normally hold myself to in the morning. Coming back to it felt like putting on a coat that had shrunk while I was gone.

That feeling is what this post is actually about. Not whether to have a routine. Not whether to abandon one for a life of pure spontaneity, which never lasted more than a few weeks for me anyway. The real question is what a routine is even for, and how much of it should stay open on purpose.

Here is what thirty years of tracking my own days has taught me. A routine that leaves no room for the unplanned is not discipline. It is a cage I built myself and then blamed for feeling trapped in.

What Structure Is Actually Buying You

A routine is not a moral position. It is a way to spend less energy on the parts of the day that do not need a decision.

1. Structure removes the small decisions so you have energy for the real ones

When the first ninety minutes of my morning are already decided, I am not negotiating with myself over whether to meditate or scroll instead. The decision was made once, months ago, and every morning since has just been carrying it out. That is what a working routine gives you.

  • It lets you get more done without needing more willpower.
  • It gives you a mindless, steady way to move through the parts of the day that do not need your full attention.
  • It helps you break a habit you actually want gone, because the new pattern is already running before the old one gets a vote.
  • It stops procrastination cold, because there is nothing left to decide.

None of that is exciting to read. It is also true, and it is the entire reason I still keep one.

The itch that shows up once the routine gets too tight

After enough months inside any fixed sequence, something in me starts to pace. I have felt it in my own practice more than once, a restlessness no amount of consistency soothes. It is closer to how an animal paces a cage than to boredom. When that feeling shows up, more discipline is not the fix. It is usually a sign the structure has stopped leaving room for anything else to happen inside it.

How I Actually Leave The Gaps

My mornings are fixed and have been for years. What changed is everything after that.

Planning the open space on purpose

I used to think a good planning session meant filling every block. Now my Sunday planning ritual does something closer to the opposite. I still write down the three or four things that actually need to happen that week. Everything else stays open on the page, not because I forgot to plan it, but because I planned for it to stay empty.

That open space is not wasted time. It is what makes room for the unplanned thing worth saying yes to, the conversation that runs long, the walk that turns into something else. None of that shows up if every hour already belongs to something.

Not fighting the pull when it comes

I no longer treat the urge to break a routine as failure. If the pull to skip the plan and do something else keeps returning after I have tried to honor the routine anyway, I take it as information instead of guilt. Usually it means I am close to changing something, not falling apart. I built the routine. I am allowed to change it when it stops serving what it was built for.

Why Roots And Room Are Not Opposites

I once heard a routine described this way. Routines root us. Routine frees the mind. I turned that over for a long time before it made sense to me, and here is where I landed. A rooted life is not a rigid one. Rootedness is exactly what makes give possible somewhere else.

Think of an actual tree. The roots hold it in place through wind that would otherwise tip it over, and it is that same holding that lets the branches move freely instead of snapping. A routine works the same way in a life. It is not there to control every hour. It is there to hold enough of the day steady that the rest of it can bend.

I keep a short list of what stays rooted no matter what. What I eat first thing. How I move my body. When the bills get paid. When I check in with the people I care about. Everything outside that short list gets to move.

Some of my best mornings started as pure accidents inside a routine that held everything else steady. A Sunday reading ritual that never changes led me to a book I would never have picked up on my own. A weekly walk on the same path put me next to a stranger I ended up talking with for an hour. None of that would have happened if I had spent that same time worrying about whether I was being productive enough.

Common Questions About Structure And Spontaneity

How do I know when to actually break my routine?

Watch for the pull that keeps returning even after you have tried to honor the routine in good faith. A single restless morning is not a signal. A restlessness that will not settle after weeks of trying probably is. Trust that one.

Will loosening my routine wreck the progress I have made?

Not if the important things were already handled before the open space showed up. The unplanned run along an unfamiliar path or the last minute trip only feels good because the logistics that made it possible were already sorted somewhere else. Space for spontaneity is not the opposite of planning. It depends on it.

What if I genuinely need more structure than most people seem to?

Then keep more of it. Some seasons of life and some temperaments need a firmer routine to feel steady at all, and there is nothing wrong with that. The point was never to have less structure. It was to leave at least one door open somewhere in it, so that when something worth walking through shows up, you actually can.
