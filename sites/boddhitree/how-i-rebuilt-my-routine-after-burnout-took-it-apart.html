How I Rebuilt My Routine After Burnout Took It Apart

By Theo Marsh · 7 November 2026

These days I keep a real day. Meals at the same three times, a walk I actually take, work that stops when it is meant to stop. None of that sounds remarkable until you have lived through a stretch where you could not manage any of it, and I could not, for the better part of two years.

Before the collapse I was the person who said yes to everything and called it dedication. I had spent years training under different teachers in Buddhist and Stoic traditions, keeping a paper journal of every habit I tried, and somehow none of it stopped me from running myself straight into the ground inside a job that never let up.

The signs were rarely dramatic at first. You keep functioning. From the outside you might even look fine, capable of showing up, remembering, helping, adapting, right up until you cannot.

Mine did not arrive as one bad week. It arrived as months of symptoms I kept explaining away, until a single ordinary Tuesday made it impossible to explain away any longer.

What I want to tell you here is not the collapse itself. It is what came after, because rebuilding a routine after burnout is not the same project as building one from a place of ordinary tiredness. The rules are different. Some of what I thought would help actually made things worse, and I only found that out by testing it on myself, the way I test everything, for months before I trusted it enough to write about it.

Burnout is not just being tired. It reflects a longer stretch of taking on too much, carrying too much feeling, and resting too little, and by the time you notice it clearly, your whole system has already gotten less tolerant of demand than it used to be.

So this is the account of how my days actually got rebuilt, not the version I would have preferred to tell you, where discipline fixed everything inside a tidy stretch of weeks.

What Burnout Actually Took Away

The googling test

There is an unglamorous shorthand I have heard used for spotting burnout, and it turned out to be true for me. You know you are in it when you find yourself typing "burnout symptoms" into a search bar at midnight, already suspecting the answer. I did that more than once before I admitted what was happening.

Green, amber, red

Once I was paying attention, I started tracking my own stress signals the way I track everything else, in the paper journal I have kept for years. I used a simple traffic light for it. Green meant everything was fine. Amber meant slow down and pay attention. Red meant a boundary had already been crossed and rest was not optional anymore.

For each color I wrote down what actually helped, so the next time amber showed up I was not guessing what to do about it. That one habit, logging the color before logging anything else, is still the first thing I do most mornings.

Not a tiredness problem

Burnout is not the same thing as being tired. It is what happens after too much responsibility, too much feeling carried for too long, and not enough real recovery, and by the time you notice it clearly your whole system has already gotten less tolerant of demand than it used to be.

This is why some of the most common advice backfires:

  • Pushing harder adds weight to a system that already needs repair, not less.
  • Waiting for motivation rarely works either, because motivation tends to return after some capacity comes back, not before it.
  • Comparing your collapse to someone else's usually just adds shame on top of exhaustion, which was never a useful trade.

How I Actually Rebuilt My Days

1. Rest first, structure second

The first stretch was not a program. It was eat, rest, sleep, repeat, with an occasional crying spell that arrived for no obvious reason and turned out to be a completely ordinary part of it. I limited my walks to fifteen or twenty minutes and moved through them at what I started calling granny speed, because anything faster left me wrecked for the rest of the day.

I want to be honest about how unglamorous this phase was. There was no morning ritual, no journaling practice, nothing that looked like the routines I usually write about here. There was only rest, taken without earning it first.

2. Structure by food, not by tasks

When I finally tried to add shape back into my days, organizing around tasks failed almost immediately. A fixed walk at four in the afternoon sounded reasonable and fell apart within a week. What actually held was structuring the day around meals instead. Breakfast, lunch, and dinner at the same times, focus heavy work in the hours after breakfast, and lighter, less demanding tasks in the afternoon, because by then I was usually fried anyway.

About a month or two into that, I started setting an alarm for eight every morning. It sounds too small to matter. It brought more calm than almost anything else I tried.

3. One percent experiments

Once some capacity returned, I stopped trying to redesign my whole life at once and started running small experiments instead, one at a time, the way I test any habit before I trust it. I would pick one of each kind and treat myself like a scientist watching for what actually helped, even by a small amount:

  • Energy experiments, like going to bed fifteen minutes earlier or taking a real lunch break three times that week.
  • Boundary experiments, like answering an email a little more briefly, or saying "I need to think about it" instead of agreeing to something on the spot.
  • Kindness experiments, like talking to myself in a softer tone for a single day, or leaving myself a supportive note somewhere I would actually see it.

At the end of each week I wrote down what had given me even one percent more energy, what had drained it, and what needed to get smaller. That weekly page is still the closest thing I have to a recovery plan.

What I Got Wrong the First Time

The three day fast

In the first month I tried a three day juice fast to speed the healing along. I do not recommend it. Restriction costs energy too, and a depleted system does not need one more thing asking something of it.

Overstepping my own limits, on repeat

For a long stretch, more than an hour with friends, more than half an hour at my desk, or a walk that ran too long would knock me flat the next day, sometimes for two. I kept testing that edge anyway, because feeling slightly better made me forget how close the edge actually was.

Expecting a straight line

I had privately decided I would beat the odds and be fully back within three months. I was warned it would probably take three times as long as I thought, and it did. Recovery is not a straight line. Some weeks I would go two steps back, and by the time I came out the other side of that dip, it usually turned out I had gone three steps forward instead.

There is a line about riding a bicycle, that you only keep your balance by continuing to move. I used to think that meant never wobble. It actually means the wobble is not proof you are doing it wrong. The wobble is what balance looks like while it is happening.

Questions I Still Get About This

How long does rebuilding a routine after burnout actually take?

Longer than you want it to. In my case, and in most of what I have read since, real recovery runs somewhere between six months and two years depending on how deep it went and how much support you have around you. The first three to six months are mostly about rest and stabilizing. The stretch after that is where new habits actually start to hold.

What if I relapse?

You probably will, at least once. I did. Treat it as information instead of failure. A relapse usually means a boundary got crossed again, not that the whole rebuild has to start over.

Do the small things actually count?

Yes. A system that has been carrying too much for too long tends to respond better to small, repeatable moves than to an ambitious overhaul, because it can actually keep up with small. That was true for me every single time I forgot it and tried the big version instead.
