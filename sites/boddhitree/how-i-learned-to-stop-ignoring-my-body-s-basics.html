How I Learned To Stop Ignoring My Body's Basics

By Theo Marsh · 24 September 2026

I used to laugh at the idea that drinking enough water and getting to bed on time counted as self care. I was eating, sleeping in some fashion, and moving through my days. Outside of the cushion and my job, I barely had energy for anything else, and I told myself that was already enough.

It took running myself into the ground to learn otherwise.

Before I hit my own wall, I looked fine from the outside. Busy, capable, on top of my responsibilities, or at least that was how it read to everyone around me. Underneath it I was skipping meals, skipping movement, skipping sleep, and skipping any kind of emotional maintenance, then wondering why life had started to feel heavy and foggy.

Most people do not burn out from one dramatic event. They burn out from slow neglect, one skipped basic at a time, until the body stops asking and starts demanding.

That demand, when it finally arrived, did not knock politely.

What My Body Was Actually Telling Me

The version of me everyone saw

I was a perfectionist and an overachiever, the type A, go getting, coffee drinking version of myself with a life that looked enviable from the outside. At least, that is how it looked. In reality I was permanently stressed, overwhelmed, exhausted, emotionally drained, and anxious. My immune system was compromised. My hair was falling out. My digestive system was crippled by stress. I rarely slept more than four hours a night, and I avoided my own friends unless there was alcohol involved to soften the conversation.

None of that showed up in the version of my life I was presenting to the world, which is exactly the problem. I had gotten so good at performing wellness that I had stopped checking in on the real thing.

When the body stops asking nicely

There is an old fable about a frog in a pan of water. Toss the frog into water that is already hot and it will leap out immediately. Put the frog in cold water and heat it slowly, and it will keep adjusting, over and over, until it cannot adjust any more and it boils without ever jumping. I spent years as that frog, adjusting to a temperature that kept climbing, telling myself I was fine because I had adjusted to it yesterday too.

Burnout was a lifetime in the making for a lot of us. Weeks of pushing too hard, overcommitting, filling every gap, running on adrenaline, ignoring what my body was telling me, saying yes when I should have said no. Then one day my body screamed loud enough that I could not drown it out any more. I could not push any further, and pretending otherwise stopped being an option.

Why I Kept Ignoring the Basics For So Long

Getting my worth from work

I did not know burnout was a thing for a long time. Being tired was normal. I got my sense of worth from how much I was producing, without ever noticing that was where it was coming from. I had one tool for managing stress, exercise, and I used it well, but that was only one piece of a much bigger picture. Eventually even that got dropped. I stopped seeing friends. I stopped answering messages. Work became my whole social life, and I lost enough weight that a coworker finally told me I did not look so good, which is never a compliment.

Running on empty and calling it normal

The pattern was always the same. Go to bed late again. Oversleep. Rush out the door on a sugary breakfast and coffee. Arrive somewhere half awake and wired at the same time, scattered, forgetting things that mattered. It looked productive and well managed from a distance. It was actually survival, and barely that.

What finally got through to me was not another productivity trick or a better calendar system. It was going back to the fundamentals I had convinced myself I had already outgrown.

The Fundamentals I Went Back To

Self care basics are not optional extras. They are the foundation everything else gets built on, and when that foundation is weak, no amount of motivation makes up for it. For me they broke down into a handful of plain, unglamorous categories: eating, moving, sleeping, and water.

Eating to support energy, not to punish myself

Eating is a biological activity, not a moral one, though I had spent years treating it like one. I ate too fast, ate distracted, ate to manage stress instead of to fuel myself, then wondered why I felt foggy and irritable by mid afternoon. The shift that actually helped was a simple question asked before eating: what would help me feel steady an hour from now, instead of what do I want right now.

Moving without needing it to be dramatic

Movement improves mood, clears mental fog, and helps regulate stress, and none of that requires intensity. Five minutes of walking counts. Gentle stretching counts. The point was never to punish my body or chase a fitness trend. It was to remind my own nervous system that I was safe and capable, instead of treating myself like a machine I could drive as hard as I wanted indefinitely.

Protecting sleep instead of negotiating it away

Sleep is the most underestimated basic there is, and for years I treated it as the first thing to sacrifice whenever anything else needed the hours. When sleep suffers, patience erodes, clarity fades, emotions get louder, and decisions get worse. I did not fix my sleep through effort. I fixed it through protection: a consistent bedtime, fewer screens late at night, and a calmer routine leading up to it.

Drinking water like it actually mattered

Hydration was the basic I ignored longest, mostly because it seemed too small to matter next to everything else on my plate. Then I learned the body is roughly sixty percent water, and started carrying a bottle with me everywhere, sipping during my morning routine and before meals instead of waiting until I was already thirsty and behind. The results were almost immediate. More energy. Better concentration. No more of that heavy after lunch slump. My irritability faded and something closer to calm took its place. It turns out something as small as drinking water on purpose can change how an entire day feels. These days that water runs through a filter from greatwaterfilters.com.au before it reaches the glass. Good-tasting water is water you actually keep drinking.

Making the Basics Actually Stick

Starting smaller than felt necessary

The basics of self care are things like waking up early enough to prepare for the day, making the bed, brushing your teeth, showering, and eating lunch. Skipping any one of these can look inconsequential in the short term, but let enough of them slide and something eventually gives. I am nowhere near perfect with any of this. There are still days I do not stick to a workout or get to bed early. What changed was that I started noticing when I had drifted, then course corrected, rather than letting the drift go unexamined for weeks.

The small actions that made the biggest difference asked almost nothing of me:

  • Drink water before coffee
  • Walk for five minutes
  • Turn a screen off thirty minutes earlier than usual
  • Take three slow breaths before responding to something stressful

None of that reads as a transformation. It reads as stabilization, and stabilization turned out to be the actual beginning of anything bigger.

Making room for the emotional basics too

Physical care supports energy. Emotional care supports stability, and without it stress just builds quietly until it comes out sideways. Setting a boundary is not selfish, it is honest, and I had to relearn that saying no to something was not a failure of character. Staying connected to other people mattered just as much. Isolation weakens resilience. A shared conversation, or simply letting someone else walk alongside me for a stretch, did more for my nervous system than any amount of willpower ever did.

What I would tell myself at the start

If I were talking to the version of me who thought eating, sleeping, and moving through the day was already enough, I would tell her to pick one physical basic and one emotional basic, make each one small enough to actually repeat, and give it a week before judging whether it worked. Do not worry about doing any of it perfectly. On plenty of days now, simply not getting out of bed for another ten minutes or turning my phone off early still counts as taking care of myself. Progress was never about intensity. It was always about showing up for the basics again and again, long after the crisis that first forced me to notice them had passed.
