My knee pain was not a reason to abandon the squat

Posted 13 July 2027 by I Prescribe Exercise

When my knee started hurting during squats, I wanted a clean rule. Either squats were bad for my knees or I had done something stupid enough to ruin the lift for myself. Both stories let me avoid looking at the harder question, which was what had changed in my training and in the way I was moving.

The pain did not feel the same every time. Sometimes it was a vague ache around the kneecap. Sometimes it was more focused below it. That alone was enough to stop me from pretending every sore knee needs the same fix.

I also stopped treating the squat as the villain. A deep, controlled squat is not automatically bad for a healthy knee. The problem is more often a load that rose faster than the tissue could handle, a position I could not control, or a restriction somewhere above or below the knee.

I look at the load before I blame the movement

A sudden increase in squat volume, intensity, or frequency can be enough to make a knee complain. I have been guilty of adding weight because the last session felt easy, then adding extra sets because I wanted progress to happen faster. My muscles may have felt ready. That did not mean the tendon or joint was ready for the same jump.

Rather than shut down all lower body work, I would now adjust the dose. That can mean less load, fewer hard sets, a slower repetition, or a depth I can control without the pain getting worse. The aim is to keep a workable version of the pattern while I build capacity back up.

The knee is not the only place to check

A squat asks the ankle, knee, and hip to share the descent. When my heel lifts, my knees fall inward, or my back rounds near the bottom, I do not just stare at the knee. Limited ankle movement can make the rest of the squat compensate. A stance that does not suit my hips can do the same.

One simple check is bringing the knee toward a wall while keeping the heel down. I compare both sides rather than chasing a perfect number. If one ankle gives me far less room, it may be changing how I reach depth under the bar.

These are the cues I return to before deciding the knee itself is the whole problem:

  • Keep my weight spread across the heel, outer edge, and base of the big toe.
  • Let the knees track in the same direction as the feet instead of collapsing inward.
  • Use a depth where I can keep control rather than forcing a position I do not own.
  • Build ankle and hip range alongside the squat when either is clearly limiting it.

I do not force my knees to stay behind my toes. Knees can travel forward in a squat. The more useful question is whether I can control the whole position with a load that fits my current ability.

I keep other leg work available

If squatting is irritating the knee, I do not need to decide that all leg training is over. A Romanian deadlift, leg press, hamstring curl, leg extension, or a split squat variation may give me a way to keep working, depending on what I can tolerate. I use the version that does not make the symptom worse and pay attention to how the knee responds afterward.

That is not an excuse to collect random substitutions forever. It is a way to keep strength work in place while I sort out the load, technique, range, or specific pain pattern that needs attention.

I do not guess through the warning signs

Locking, catching, giving way, swelling that keeps returning, pain at rest or at night, and pain that keeps climbing despite sensible changes are reasons to get assessed. A knee that cannot bear weight, becomes hot and red, or hurts after a fall or twist also needs proper medical attention.

I want to keep squatting, but not by turning every painful repetition into a test of stubbornness. The better return is built from a tolerable dose, a position I can control, and enough patience to let the knee catch up to the work I am asking it to do.
