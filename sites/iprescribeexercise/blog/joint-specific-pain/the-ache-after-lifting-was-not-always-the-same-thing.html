The ache after lifting was not always the same thing

Posted 10 September 2026 by I Prescribe Exercise

I used to put every sore lower back in the same mental box. Training is hard. Deadlifts are hard. A bit of tightness must mean I had worked hard enough.

Then I started noticing a difference. A dull ache that arrived a day or two after a demanding session and eased as I moved was one thing. A local pinch that showed up during a set, or immediately after the same lift every time, was another. I had been calling both of them soreness because that was easier than changing what I was doing.

My lower back was doing more work than I had admitted.

When the back takes over

In a hinge, squat, or press, the spine needs to stay steady while the hips and legs do the moving. When I rounded or overarched through the lower back, it became the part absorbing the load. The same thing happened when I went into an overhead press with a sagging middle. My back extended to make up for a position I had not earned.

Years of sitting did not help. Tight hip flexors, a pelvis already tipped, sleepy glutes, and a rushed start to the session left the lower back ready to volunteer before I had even touched a serious weight.

I stopped treating that as a character flaw or a reason to panic. It was feedback. It told me to look at the movement and the amount of load before deciding I had damaged something.

The changes I could actually repeat

I did not need an elaborate recovery routine. I needed to stop asking my back to do the hips and core work for them. Before working sets, I started with a breath into my belly and a gentle brace through the middle, as if I was about to be poked. Lighter sets gave me a chance to feel whether I could keep that brace.

For hinges, I practised pushing my hips back toward the wall behind me, keeping the spine in one neutral line, then driving the hips forward to stand. If I could not tell where the movement was coming from, the weight was too heavy for the lesson I was trying to learn.

My preparation became more specific:

  • Slow cat cow movements to get the spine moving
  • A kneeling hip flexor stretch before lifting
  • Glute bridges that came from the hips rather than a big lower back arch
  • Controlled dead bug and bird dog work for the trunk
  • Lighter practice sets before loading the main lift

None of that is exciting. It is also more useful than repeatedly testing a sore back with the lift that irritated it.

What I no longer train through

Gentle movement and an easier session can make more sense than lying still. A short walk and easy mobility are different from piling another heavy session onto an irritated back the next day. I can keep training the parts that do not reproduce the pain while I change the pattern that does.

There are lines I do not cross by myself. Numbness, tingling, weakness spreading down a leg, bladder or bowel changes, fever, a major trauma, a distinct pop under load, or pain that is severe or getting worse all deserve proper assessment. So does pain that does not settle after easier training.

I still want to lift. The next useful decision is not to prove that I can ignore an ache. It is to make sure my hips, glutes, and trunk are doing enough of the work that my lower back can stop trying to do all of it.
