The warnings I used to call normal

Posted 30 September 2026 by I Prescribe Exercise

Overuse did not arrive as one dramatic injury for me. It arrived as a run that felt flat, a joint that stayed stiff after it should have settled, and the strange effort of talking myself into a session I normally wanted to do.

I used to call all of that normal training. I thought discipline meant following the plan even when the plan no longer matched the body doing it. That was a convenient story because it meant I did not have to reduce volume, skip a hard session, or admit I was underrecovered.

Eventually the small warnings stopped being small.

The signs were not only pain

Persistent or worsening pain is an obvious reason to pay attention, especially when it changes how I move. A sharp pain, swelling, limping, pain at rest, or pinpoint bone pain is not a test of toughness. I stop and get it looked at.

But the earlier signs can be quieter. I now take note when I am sluggish on an easy session, when a familiar pace suddenly takes more effort, or when soreness lasts several days instead of fading. A higher than usual resting heart rate, a drop in my usual heart rate variability, restless sleep, irritability, poor focus, and a loss of appetite after hard training can all sit beside the physical signals.

The one that fooled me most was motivation. Everybody has an off day. Dreading every workout, feeling flat in a session, or having to drag myself out the door for more than a passing rough patch was information, not laziness.

Why more work was not the answer

Training asks the tissues to adapt. Bones, muscles, tendons, joints, and ligaments are stressed, repaired, and made ready for more. Trouble starts when I add the next hard effort before that repair process has caught up.

It can happen gradually through too little recovery between demanding sessions. It can also happen after one session that exceeds what the body can currently handle. The problem is not simply that I used my body too much. It is that the load exceeded my ability to recover from it.

Other stress counts too. Work, poor sleep, family demands, and a disrupted routine can change what I am ready to absorb even if the numbers in my training plan have not changed.

What I change before an injury makes the choice for me

I have become less impressed by a plan on paper. If the signals are stacking up, I change the session before the body forces a longer break.

  • I reduce intensity or overall volume instead of adding more to catch up
  • I change one training variable at a time so I can see how I respond
  • I use an easier session or a rest day when fatigue is carrying from one workout to the next
  • I build strength and mobility work into the week rather than treating them as an afterthought
  • I return gradually after pain settles instead of trying to make up missed training

There is no single formula that applies to every person. The useful part is learning what my normal feels like, then noticing when that normal has changed. I would rather take one or two recovery days now than turn a manageable warning into weeks away from training.

The next session is still there

Backing off once felt like falling behind. I see it differently now. The aim is not to survive one heroic workout. It is to finish a hard session with enough left to recover and train again.

I still have to remind myself of that when I am on a roll. The warning signs are not an accusation. They are a chance to adjust while the decision is still mine.
