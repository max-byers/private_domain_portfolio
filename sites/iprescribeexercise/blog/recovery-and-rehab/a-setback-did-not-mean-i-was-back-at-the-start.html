A setback did not mean I was back at the start

Posted 17 September 2027 by I Prescribe Exercise

The part of recovery I still find hardest is when something starts to go well, then suddenly does not. A few comfortable sessions can make me feel as though the problem is behind me. One bad response after that can make me wonder whether all the earlier work counted for nothing.

That thinking has pushed me into both directions before. I have tried to test the painful area too soon because I wanted certainty. I have also treated a flare as proof that I should stop everything. Neither reaction gave me much patience.

A setback can be frustrating without meaning that I am back at the beginning. It is information about what the body handled, what it did not, and what needs to change next.

I stopped turning every bad day into a verdict

Injury recovery can bring a lot of rigid thinking. If I cannot do the session exactly as planned, it is tempting to call the day a failure. If a movement feels different, I can read it as a sign that I will never trust it again. Fear of another injury makes every unfamiliar sensation feel important.

That reaction is understandable, especially when training has been one of the ways I deal with stress. But sleep, outside worries, fatigue, and the pressure to get back quickly can all affect how recovery feels. I try to step back before I decide what one difficult day means.

I change the target before I change the effort

When the plan is not working, I do not always need to abandon training. I may need to change the target. A runner can keep some fitness with swimming or pool running while land running is not ready. Someone returning to a sport can work on drills and technique before full sparring or full intensity. The replacement does not recreate every part of the original activity, but it can keep a useful piece of the routine in place.

My own version is simpler. If the movement I wanted to do has become too much, I look for work that is still manageable. I lower the demand, use another exercise, or train a different area. That gives me something productive to do without pretending the setback is not there.

I make the next step small enough to trust

The urge after a better day is to make up for lost time. That is where I have often created the next problem. A return needs controlled increments, not a leap from a cautious session straight into a hard test.

  • Keep the first return session easier than my confidence wants it to be.
  • See how the area responds later that day and the next morning.
  • Bring back intensity gradually instead of adding every missing piece at once.
  • Adjust the goal if the timeline has changed.

Changing a goal is not the same as giving up on it. I may need to aim for a comfortable training session rather than a personal best, or for a return to practice rather than the hardest version of it. That adjustment gives the next decision a realistic shape.

I keep the setback in proportion

Recovery is not only physical. I can feel isolated when I am missing the work that normally helps me feel like myself. Talking honestly with the people around me, keeping contact with the training environment when I can, and getting help for anxiety or low mood can be part of the process too.

I still dislike setbacks. I do not need to call them useful to take them seriously. But I can stop making them a catastrophe. The next good decision is usually smaller and less dramatic than I want it to be, which is often why it gives me the best chance of training again next week.
