Being cleared was not the same as being ready

Posted 9 February 2027 by I Prescribe Exercise

Finishing rehabilitation felt like a green light. I had less pain, more movement, and a list of exercises that had helped. I wanted to go back to the lifts that made me feel like myself and see whether the old numbers were still there.

That urge is understandable. It is also where I have made the return harder than it needed to be. Being able to walk, carry things, or complete the last rehabilitation session did not mean I had rebuilt the capacity for repeated heavy work.

Cleared was the start of the next phase, not the end of the process.

I kept the useful parts of rehabilitation

The small exercises that helped me regain control did not become pointless when the pain reduced. Balance work, controlled single side movements, trunk work, and the other drills that showed me where I compensated still had a place at the start of a session.

After injury, the body can get good at protecting itself. I might shift away from one side, stiffen a nearby joint, or change how I squat, hinge, or press without noticing. Those strategies can be helpful early on, but they become a problem if I load them without first getting control back.

Before I chase intensity, I check whether I can move through the basics with reasonable control. For a lower body return, that might mean stairs, a controlled squat, a split squat, or a hinge. For a shoulder, it might mean comfortable reach, a stable pressing position, and pulling strength. I use those movements to find a starting point, not to prove I am finished.

I rebuilt capacity before testing strength

Capacity is my ability to tolerate repeated work, recover from it, and turn up ready for the next session. It is different from managing one strong repetition on a good day. I can feel strong before the joint, tendon, and supporting muscles are prepared for the same workload they handled before the injury.

So I begin with a version of the pattern that I can control. If a conventional deadlift irritates my back, a hinge from a raised height, kettlebell deadlift, or another manageable variation may let me train the same general pattern. If a back squat is not ready, a goblet squat, split squat, or box squat can keep the legs working. I keep the rest of the body as active as the situation allows instead of shrinking my entire training life around one area.

I change one demand at a time

Load is not the only way to make training harder. Range, repetitions, sets, tempo, frequency, speed, and exercise complexity all increase the challenge. Trying to improve several of them at once is how a careful return becomes another flare up.

My progression is deliberately plain:

  • Use a light variation with controlled form
  • Add repetitions or a small amount of load after the response has been good
  • Build the range or reduce support later if the movement needs it
  • Return to the more demanding version only when the earlier step is reliable

I use the following day as feedback. A stable feeling that settles quickly can tell me the dose was manageable. Sharp pain, new swelling, instability, a large change in form, or symptoms that are clearly worse the next day tell me to adjust the plan.

Confidence has to catch up too

Fear after injury is not solved by pretending the concern is silly. It changes when I have enough controlled repetitions to see that I can move, load, recover, and return again. That is why a first session back under a bar does not need to be a test day.

I want to become capable of the training I care about, not merely pain free enough to rush back into it. The slower return has given me something better than a quick win. It has given me a way to train that I trust.
