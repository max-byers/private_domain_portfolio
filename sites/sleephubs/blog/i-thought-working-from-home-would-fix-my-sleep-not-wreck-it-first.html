I Thought Working From Home Would Fix My Sleep, Not Wreck It First

Sleep Hubs · 9 November 2027

I Thought Working From Home Would Fix My Sleep, Not Wreck It First

My first Monday working from home, I did not set an alarm, because there was truly no reason to. My laptop was already sitting on the kitchen table. No commute, no one to see me before ten in the morning, nothing standing between me and staying under the covers for as long as I wanted. I slept until nine, made coffee in my pajamas, and told myself this was the dream I had been chasing since my night shift years.

I was wrong within about three weeks, but not in the way I expected. I did not lose sleep because I was anxious about work. I lost it because I had quietly given up every structure that used to tell my body when the day started and stopped. I used to think shift work broke my sleep because someone else controlled my schedule. Working from home broke it because nobody controlled it at all, including me.

The two problems, it turns out, come from the same root. Your body does not care whether the chaos is handed to you by an employer or built by you at your own kitchen table. It just wants the same handful of signals, at roughly the same time, every day. I spent close to a year relearning that, as a parent whose kids do not run on a remote work schedule and never will.

What Working From Home Actually Does To Your Sleep

  1. The commute was doing more work than I gave it credit for. Before I worked from home, walking to the car or the train forced me outside and gave my day a fixed start and stop time whether I liked it or not. Once that was gone, I realized how much of my old routine had been built around that structure and not around any real discipline of my own. Getting that time back sounds like a gift, and it can be, but for me it mostly meant more hours to fill with no clear signal for when to be done.
  2. Flexibility without a fixed start time turns into no schedule at all. In my first few months I told myself I would just work whenever I felt sharpest. In practice that meant going to bed later because nothing forced an early morning, sleeping in later to compensate, then going to bed even later the next night because I had woken up later that day. Close to four in ten remote workers report never getting the recommended hours of sleep, and I believe every one of them, because it took about three weeks of this drifting before I noticed my bedtime had slid nearly two hours past where it started.
  3. Working from bed ruins the bed. I told myself answering a few morning emails from under the covers was harmless. It was not. Once your brain links a space with laptops, deadlines, and a glowing screen, it stops treating that same space as somewhere to switch off, and that confusion follows you every night after, not just the mornings you actually worked from there.
  4. The isolation hits your sleep before it hits your mood. I did not expect a quiet house to keep me up at night, but going days without seeing another adult besides my own reflection in a video call window started showing up as restlessness at bedtime long before I noticed I felt lonely during the day. Feeling isolated and sleeping poorly feed each other, and once you are inside that loop it is hard to tell which one started first.
  5. Daylight and movement disappear faster than you notice. I used to get sunlight and steps just by existing, walking to the car, walking through a building, walking back. Once my whole job fit inside one room, my daily step count dropped by roughly half without me deciding to change anything, and I was going full days without direct sunlight unless I made a point of stepping outside for it. Sunlight is the loudest cue your circadian rhythm has, and I had quietly cut myself off from most of it.

How My Sleep Actually Fell Apart, And What Put It Back Together

The First Few Weeks I Ran On FREEDOM Instead Of A Plan

One week I would wake at eight thirty, make coffee, and walk around the block just to feel like I had gone somewhere before starting work. The next week I would wake at nine after two hours of scrolling in bed and be lucky if I opened my laptop by ten. Some mornings I woke up in a panic at five because I had a meeting at eight, then somehow slept straight through it and had to blame a bad internet connection, which was believable often enough that nobody questioned it twice. I kept my camera off in most calls and paced the apartment while I listened, half working, half not really there at all.

None of it felt like a problem yet. It felt like freedom. I did not notice that freedom without a shape is just chaos with better pajamas.

The Slump Where My Days And Nights STOPPED Meaning Anything

By month two, I was still logging six or seven hours of actual work, but it was smeared across the whole day instead of contained inside it. I answered emails at seven in the morning because they had come in overnight, then answered more at eight or nine at night just to get them out of the way. There was no real line where work stopped, which meant there was no real line where rest started either.

The quiet got to me before I admitted it out loud. I went stretches of two or three days without a real conversation with another adult who was not my partner, and I told myself that was fine because I was getting so much done. I was not getting more done. I was just awake for more of the day, doing less with each hour of it, and lying in bed at night with a head that would not settle even though my body was worn out. I had also stopped moving the way I used to without thinking about it. No walk to the car, no stairs at the office, nothing except a short trip from the bed to the desk and back again.

What Finally Got My CLOCK Working Again

The fix was almost insultingly boring. I picked one wake time and kept it, weekends included, whether or not I had anywhere to be. The moment I got up, I opened the curtains and stepped outside for a few minutes before I let myself touch my phone, coffee in hand, watching the street wake up instead of watching a screen wake up first. I moved my laptop off the nightstand and out of the bedroom completely, so the only thing that room was allowed to mean to my body anymore was sleep.

I gave myself an actual stopping time for work, the same one most days, and stopped answering anything after it unless the building was on fire. If I needed a nap in the afternoon, I kept it short, gone before three in the afternoon, so it would not eat into the sleep I needed that night. I started colour coding my calendar and doing my hardest thinking in the morning hours when I actually had some, saving the small mindless tasks for the slump after lunch instead of forcing focus I did not have. Twice a day, once in the morning and again after lunch, I made myself step outside for a few minutes, mostly for the light, partly just to remember what my street looked like in daylight.

The Routine That Actually STUCK

None of what worked was clever. A fixed wake time. Real boundaries on when the workday ends. A short walk outside, twice, whether I felt like it or not. A bedroom that only means sleep, with my laptop banned from it entirely. Once I stopped treating remote work as an excuse to have no schedule and started treating it like a schedule I finally got to design myself, my sleep stopped being the thing I sacrificed for the flexibility, and became the thing the flexibility was supposed to protect in the first place.

I still work from that same kitchen table most days, and I still love not commuting. But I do not trust myself with an unstructured day anymore, not after watching what one did to my sleep in a few short months. If you work from home, has your own schedule ever quietly worked against you the same way mine did, and what finally got it back under control?
