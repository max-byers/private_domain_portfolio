Two Kids, Two Years, and the Sleep Schedule That Never Once Worked

Sleep Hubs · 18 September 2027

Two Kids, Two Years, and the Sleep Schedule That Never Once Worked

The book promised eight weeks. Two weeks after my son was born, my husband and I were barely sleeping five hours a day and were desperate enough to test drive the plan that same week. It was simple on paper. Feed him enough during the day so he'd be less hungry at night, hit certain windows at certain hours, and by week eight we'd have our nights back. I remember thinking, how hard can it be.

It fell apart before lunch on day one.

That was the first time I understood something I'd spend the next two years relearning over and over. The exhaustion was never really about whether I could follow a schedule. It was about the fact that my son wasn't a schedule. He was a person, and persons don't hit their marks on cue, no matter how many milestone charts a pediatrician's office has taped to the wall.

What I Wish Someone Had Told Me First

  1. The schedule was never really the point. Two days into the book's plan, my son wanted a bottle an hour after his last one, and I sat there scrolling my tracker app trying to figure out what was wrong with him for eating that often. Nothing was wrong with him. Babies are humans, not robots, and no chart was going to change that.
  2. Sleeping through the night means five hours, not twelve. I didn't learn that until his first year was nearly over. I had been measuring every single night against a twelve hour stretch, when the real definition of sleeping through the night is five hours, and twelve doesn't usually show up until toddlerhood.
  3. A timer changes what five minutes feels like. The first time I actually clocked how long he cried before settling, instead of guessing in the dark, I was floored to find out it was seven minutes, not the eternity my body had convinced me it was.
  4. The exhaustion doesn't end when the newborn stage does. My oldest woke every one to three hours for nineteen months straight. Years later, on a rough night with my youngest, I'd still catch myself flinching at the sound of the same lullaby light projector, like my body remembered something my brain had tried to forget.
  5. Sometimes the sleep problem was never about sleep training at all. With my youngest, months of night waking eventually traced back to a dairy intolerance, not a habit, a phase, or anything a book could have talked me through.

The Two Years, In Order

THE THREE HOURS I Still Regret

By three and a half months, we were desperate enough to try letting him cry it out. The books said most babies need ten minutes or so before falling asleep on their own, and the number one rule was to never go back in once you started. So we didn't. He screamed for three hours straight while we sat in the basement playing board games with the monitor turned down, and I honestly couldn't tell which ached more, my full breasts or my heart. It never worked the way it was supposed to. It just stopped, eventually, on its own, and we never let him cry that long again.

THE SCHEDULE Fell Apart By One O'Clock

By 7am on day one, he woke up right on cue, and I put him down for his ninety minutes of wake time feeling like a genius. By 9am he ate and went back to sleep, still on track. Then at 11am, a full hour before the book said he was allowed to wake, he woke anyway and cried through every attempt to rock him back down. We limped to noon, fed him, started the ninety minutes again. By 1pm all hell broke loose. My tracker app said he'd eaten an hour earlier and could not possibly be hungry, so we tried toys, tummy time, the bouncy seat. Nothing worked until we gave in and fed him again, and I spent the rest of the afternoon wondering what was wrong with a baby who wanted to eat that often. We kept trying for two more days before we gave up completely.

NINETEEN MONTHS of the Same Three Songs

My oldest woke every one to three hours for nineteen months. He needed rocking and nursing and patting every single time, and the second I shifted him even an inch he'd wake back up. His average wake time for the day settled around 5:30am and mostly still has. By the time my youngest was six months old and sleeping in five hour stretches on her own, I thought we were done with that particular kind of tired. Then she hit seven or eight months and started crying for hours on end, right around when I went back to work part time, and I had flashbacks so strong I started to wonder if sleep deprivation PTSD might be a real diagnosis somewhere. Leaning over a crib at 2am listening to the same three lullaby songs I'd listened to with my first kid five years earlier will do that to a person.

WHAT WAS ACTUALLY Keeping Her Up

A week before her first birthday, I turned to my husband and told him I didn't know how much longer I could do this. The next day at work, a coworker asked how I was doing and I started crying in front of her, the kind where crying about crying makes you cry even harder. She put a doctor's number in my hand and told me to call that day and be honest about how urgent it was. The doctor took me seriously, ran a few non invasive tests, and when nothing conclusive turned up, told me to look at diet. It turned out her trouble sleeping had started right around when we stopped breastfeeding and switched her to a bottle. We skipped that bottle one night and gave her dairy free milk instead. She slept through the night that whole week. She turned out to be lactose intolerant the entire time, and it took me nearly a year and a full breakdown at work to notice the pattern that had been sitting in front of me the whole time.

SOME NIGHTS, I Am Lucky

These days both kids mostly sleep through the night in their own beds, and I still don't think it was luck. It was the timer instead of the guessing. It was learning that a schedule is a suggestion, not a contract a baby signed. It was noticing that the crying finally had a real cause instead of assuming I was doing something wrong. But some nights, one of them still creeps down the hall in the dark, little hands patting my face until my eyes open, and I pull them into the tangle of blankets instead of walking them back to their own room. I used to think that meant I hadn't finished the job. Now I think it just means we're both still learning how to rest. How long did it take your own house to find its version of sleeping through the night, and did anyone ever warn you it might take years instead of weeks?
