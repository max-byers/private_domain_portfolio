The Night I Finally Asked What Was Actually Keeping Me Awake

Sleep Hubs · 29 January 2027

The Night I Finally Asked What Was Actually Keeping Me Awake

There was a stretch of months, not long after I'd left night shifts behind for good, when I built up a full client roster almost by accident. More clients meant more admin, more calls that ran long, more sessions moved around to fit someone else's schedule instead of mine. I was constantly available on my phone the way I used to be constantly available for a pager, and it never once occurred to me that the two things might be doing the same damage to my sleep.

My body noticed before my brain did. Headaches that wouldn't quit. A dull ache low in my back that no amount of stretching seemed to touch. My mind felt cloudy most of the day, distracted, hard to focus on anything for more than a few minutes at a stretch. I'd sit down to plan a client's program and catch myself rereading the same paragraph three times. Then at night, the exhaustion that had flattened me all day would somehow vanish the second my head hit the pillow, replaced by a highlight reel of everything I hadn't finished. The email I forgot to send. The comment I made in a call that probably landed wrong. The vague, low grade sense that I was letting someone down somewhere.

I'd spent years learning to sleep on command for night shifts, so this felt like a personal insult. Exhausted enough to fall asleep standing up, and somehow wide awake the moment it actually mattered. I decided to stop pushing through it and actually pay attention to what was happening in my head at eleven at night, instead of white knuckling my way to morning and blaming it on a bad week.

What Actually Helped Once The Job Started Keeping Me Up

  1. Being tired and being able to sleep turned out to be two completely different things. I kept waiting for exhaustion to just knock me out. It never did. My body was worn down and my mind was still scanning for threats that didn't exist anymore, which is a strange, uncomfortable place to be lying in the dark.
  2. The very last email of the day undid the whole evening. I used to think checking my inbox once more before bed was harmless, just tying up a loose end. It wasn't harmless. One quick reply to a client at ten at night and I'd be lying there twenty minutes later with my heart rate climbing, running through everything else still sitting in that inbox.
  3. Asking myself what was actually still awake worked better than trying to force myself to sleep. Some nights it really was the work. Other nights it had nothing to do with the job at all, it was a conversation with my partner, or something one of the kids said at dinner. Once I could actually name what was running the show, it got easier to sit with it instead of just lying there fighting it.
  4. It was never really about how many hours I was working. It was about how supported I felt while I was working them. The weeks I felt like I was handling everything alone were the weeks my sleep fell apart, even when my actual workload wasn't any heavier than usual. The weeks I had someone to hand things off to, my sleep held up fine.
  5. Working from home fixed one problem and created another. I got more hours back by cutting out a commute, and on paper I had more time available for sleep. But without a door to physically walk out of, work stopped ending. It just quietly bled into the evening until I couldn't tell where the day had actually finished.
  6. A bad night stopped being an emergency once I stopped treating it like one. I used to lie there doing the math on how wrecked tomorrow was going to be. Now if I have a rough night, I remind myself I've survived plenty of them before, and that alone takes enough pressure off that I usually drop off faster.
  7. Slow breathing before bed did more for me than anything else I tried. Nothing fancy, just counting it out, in for a slow count and out even slower. It's the closest thing I've found to a manual off switch for a mind that's been running all day.

How The Worst Of It Actually Played Out

The Months I Was Running On FUMES

I won't pretend the early stretch was anything other than rough. I was bone tired by four in the afternoon most days, the kind of tired where holding a normal conversation took real effort. And yet by eleven at night, lying in bed, I was wide awake in every way that mattered. My chest felt tight. My thoughts wouldn't slow down enough to land on any one thing before jumping to the next. I'd fall asleep eventually out of sheer depletion, wake up still feeling like I'd barely closed my eyes, and do the whole thing again the next day.

Nothing about my evening routine had changed. Same dark room, same reasonable bedtime, same phone charging across the room instead of next to the bed. None of it mattered because the problem was never the room. It was everything still running in my head.

The Night I Asked What Was Actually AWAKE

The shift started on an ordinary night, nothing dramatic about it. I was lying there again, tense for no obvious reason, and instead of gritting my teeth through it I asked myself, almost out loud, what was actually still awake in me right now. Not what I should be worried about. What actually was awake.

Some nights the honest answer was work. A client I was worried I'd let down. A message I hadn't replied to. Other nights it wasn't work at all, it was something smaller and more personal that had nothing to do with my job but had been sitting there unattended all day because work had taken up all the room. Once I actually asked the question instead of just lying there in a fog of general dread, I noticed the reasons changed from night to night. Which meant the fix couldn't be the same every time either.

Getting My SUPPORT Right Mattered As Much As Getting My Hours Right

The real turning point wasn't a schedule change. It was admitting I'd been trying to run the whole thing on my own. I started actually telling my partner when a week was piling up instead of quietly gritting my teeth through it, and I started handing off admin work I'd been white knuckling through alone at night instead of during business hours where it belonged. The weeks that followed, even when the workload itself hadn't dropped, felt completely different to fall asleep after.

I also finally set a hard cutoff for my phone and my inbox, somewhere around nine, so the last thing I did before bed stopped being a reply to someone else's problem. It sounds small written down. It was not small in practice.

Where I Landed, And Why One Bad Night Doesn't Wreck Me Anymore

These days I'm sleeping somewhere around seven to eight hours most nights, tracked on the same watch that used to depress me during the caffeine years. I get sick less often than I used to, which I only really noticed in hindsight. I feel steadier during the day, less likely to snap at my kids over something small, more able to actually be present for a conversation instead of half listening while running through my own head.

None of that means every night is perfect. Some nights I still lie there for a while. The difference is I no longer treat that as proof that something is broken. I trust that most nights will be fine, and that's enough to make most nights actually fine.

If work has been the thing keeping you up, what's the actual honest answer when you stop and ask yourself what's still awake in your head right now?

If you want somewhere to start, the breathing trick costs nothing and takes about two minutes. In through the nose for a count of four, out through the mouth for a count of seven, repeated slowly until your shoulders drop. Pair it with a real cutoff time for your phone and your inbox, even just an hour before bed, and give it more than one night before deciding it isn't working. It took me weeks before I trusted it, not days.
