Moving My Workout To The Morning Changed How Fast I Fall Asleep

For about two years my only real workout window was 8pm, after the kids were down and the day's meetings were finally over. Squat rack in the garage, eight or ten hard sets, done by nine. I liked how it felt in the moment, the heavy breathing, the pump, the sense of having earned the day. What I didn't like was what came next. I would shower, brush my teeth, get into bed by ten, and then lie there for forty minutes or more with my heart still going like I'd finished the set two minutes earlier instead of an hour.

I spent years on rotating shifts learning the hard way that your body treats light, food, and activity as signals, not just background noise. Once I got back onto a normal schedule, I assumed I'd left that lesson behind me. But watching my sleep tracker log the same slow, restless start to the night after every evening workout made it obvious I hadn't actually applied what I knew. I had a background in exercise science telling me exactly why a hard lifting session raises your core body temperature and dumps cortisol and adrenaline into your system right when you're supposed to be cooling down for bed, and I kept doing it anyway because 8pm was the only slot that existed.

So instead of reading one more article about it, I moved the whole routine to 6am for three weeks and tracked exactly one number every night: how long it actually took me to fall asleep, not how tired I felt or how good the sleep looked the next morning. That number is what changed, and it changed faster than I expected.

What Changed Once I Moved My Workout To Mornings

  1. Falling asleep stopped taking forty minutes. My tracker had been logging thirty five to forty five minutes to fall asleep on evening lifting days for months. Within the first week of morning sessions, that number dropped to twelve to eighteen minutes most nights, and it stayed there.
  2. My body temperature wasn't fighting me at bedtime anymore. A hard workout raises your core temperature and heart rate for hours afterward, which works directly against the drop in temperature your body needs to fall asleep. Moving that spike to 6am meant it had settled hours before ten o'clock instead of still climbing right as I was trying to wind down.
  3. The cortisol was doing something useful instead of keeping me wired. Cortisol is naturally at its highest right when you wake up. Training during that window seems to make use of the spike instead of adding a second one late at night, and it stays lower for a full day or two afterward, not just for an hour.
  4. I was noticeably more patient with my kids by the second week. This one surprised me. Exercise is tied to how the body regulates mood chemicals like serotonin, and by day eight or nine I noticed I wasn't snapping at my daughter over the usual morning chaos the way I had been for months.
  5. A ten o'clock curfew showed up without me trying to build one. Setting a 6am alarm meant staying up late scrolling or half watching something just to feel like I'd had time to myself stopped being worth it. My body started shutting down closer to nine thirty on its own, which was the actual goal the whole time.
  6. Ten minutes of morning sunlight did more than I expected. Training outside or near a window in the morning appears to push melatonin production later in the evening, which shortens the time it takes to fall asleep that night. The mornings I trained outside beat the mornings I trained in the garage, even if only by a little.
  7. The evening workouts weren't actually the problem, the timing was. Moderate sessions finished two to four hours before bed don't seem to disrupt sleep much at all. What was hurting me specifically was lifting heavy less than an hour before I tried to fall asleep, night after night, for two years straight.

How It Actually Went, Week By Week

The First Few Mornings Were Just ROUGH

I won't dress this part up. The first few 6am alarms were miserable. My body wanted the extra hour of sleep more than it wanted a workout, and I dragged myself through sets that felt heavier than they should have. Twice that first week I nearly went back to my old 8pm slot out of pure habit, standing in the garage at six in the morning wondering why I was doing this to myself.

By Day Five The OLD SCHEDULE Kept Pulling Me Back

A late meeting and a sick kid pushed me back to an 8pm session twice that week, and both nights handed me exactly the comparison I needed without meaning to. Both times, my tracker logged over thirty minutes to fall asleep again, right back to where I'd started. Lining those two nights up against the five morning workouts around them made the pattern impossible to argue with, even to myself.

Week Two Was The TURNING POINT

This is where it actually started to feel automatic instead of forced. Falling asleep was consistently under twenty minutes. I was waking up at 6am without dreading it the way I had the first week, and my afternoon energy stopped crashing the way it used to around three o'clock. My patience with my kids kept improving too, to the point where my partner asked what had changed.

By Week Three I Wasn't Setting An Alarm For Bed Anymore

By the last week, I didn't need a reminder to wind down. I was tired, actually tired, by nine thirty most nights, and asleep not long after my head hit the pillow. The forty minute nights from two years of 8pm lifting sessions felt like a different person's problem by then.

Would I have believed any of this if someone had told me three weeks earlier? Probably not. I'd read the same studies telling me evening exercise wasn't supposed to be the villain, and mostly it wasn't. It was specifically ending a hard session less than an hour before bed that was wrecking my nights, not exercise itself. If you're lying awake after your evening workout the same way I used to, have you actually tested whether it's the exercise or just the timing?

If mornings genuinely aren't an option for you, you don't have to give up training to fix this. Finishing a moderate session two to four hours before bed doesn't seem to cause the same problem, so shifting a lifting session from eight to six can be enough on its own. Save anything high intensity, intervals, heavy sets, for earlier in the day if you can, and if evening really is your only window, follow it with a proper cooldown and a cool shower so your body isn't still running hot when you try to fall asleep.
