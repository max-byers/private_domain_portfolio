I Started Treating My Commute Like Part Of My Sleep Routine, Not Time I Lost Before It

My drive home used to run about forty five minutes when everything went right, which it almost never did. An accident up ahead, a stalled train blocking the crossing, black ice on the exit ramp, and forty five minutes turned into an hour and a half more nights than I want to count. I would sit there watching the brake lights stack up in front of me, doing the math on dinner, on getting everyone settled, on whether I would even see the rest of my house awake once I finally walked in.

For years I blamed the exhaustion on the drive itself. Long day, long drive, of course I was tired. What I never clocked was that the drive was not only tiring me out. It was quietly eating the only buffer I had left before bed, the stretch of evening where a person eats something, decompresses, and actually gets sleepy on schedule instead of dropping into a mattress wired and starving at ten at night.

So I stopped treating the commute as dead time I just had to survive and started paying attention to what it was actually doing to my sleep, then testing whether I could change any of it without quitting my job or moving house. I tracked it for a month. Here is what actually moved the needle, and what I could never fully fix.

What Actually Moved The Needle

  1. The drive does not wind you down, it winds you up. Even on the calmer nights, my body stayed in what I can only call watch mode the whole way home. Traffic, headlights, constant stopping and starting keep your nervous system switched on for the entire ride, so you arrive home worn out physically while your brain is still fully alert. That gap between a tired body and an alert brain is exactly what makes it so hard to fall asleep once you are finally in bed.
  2. Sleep is the first thing that gets squeezed. Lose an hour or more to the road and something in the evening has to give. For me it was always sleep. I got home later, ate later, started winding down later, then either forced myself into bed too late or tried to rush into sleep before my body was actually ready for it. Neither one worked.
  3. Forty five minutes a day is apparently the line. I always assumed only the brutal two hour commutes counted as a real problem. A commute that runs longer than forty five minutes a day is enough on its own to drag sleep quality down, and the risk climbs fast once a long commute sits on top of a long work week. People working over fifty two hours a week with more than two hours of daily commuting carry a meaningfully higher risk of insomnia than people who work a normal week and commute under an hour. Shift workers with long commutes carry that same extra risk.
  4. The stress does not clock out when you park the car. Sitting in traffic every day triggers the same stress response as any other threat, cortisol and adrenaline flooding in to get you ready to react to something. Do that daily and it becomes chronic. Car commuters in particular report higher stress and moodiness than people who walk, bike, or take public transit, mostly because you are stuck alone with the frustration with nowhere to put it until you are already home.
  5. You cannot make up the difference on a Saturday. I used to tell myself a long lie in on the weekend would even things out. It does not work that way. Sleep debt from a week of long commutes does not get erased by one good weekend, which means the actual fix has to happen on the weeknights themselves, not after the fact.
  6. How you get there matters almost as much as how long it takes. Driving turned out to be the most stressful way to commute of all the options. People who walk or bike to work report feeling more energized when they arrive than people who drove or took transit, even in bad weather. I could not walk to my job, but that finding is part of why changing how I drove ended up mattering more than I expected.

The Month I Actually Tracked It

The First Week, I Just Watched Myself Get WIRED For No Reason

I did not change anything the first week. I just wrote down what time I actually got home, what time I ate, what time I gave up and went to bed, and how I felt lying there. It was not pretty. Most nights I walked in somewhere between six thirty and seven fifteen, ate standing at the counter because sitting down felt like too much of a commitment, and did not start winding down until close to nine.

The worst part was how wired I felt despite being genuinely exhausted. My body wanted to collapse. My brain would not stop replaying the drive, a near miss on the highway, a driver who cut me off, the radio host arguing about something I did not even care about. I reached for my phone almost every night as a reward for surviving the day, which of course kept me up even later.

Week Two, I Made The Drive Itself Less ACTIVATING

This is where I actually started changing things. I swapped the news and the talk radio for calmer music and audiobooks, nothing that raised my heart rate before I had even parked. I adjusted my seat so my shoulders and lower back had real support instead of just tolerating the position for forty five minutes. On the days the drive ran long, I took a short break instead of pushing straight through, since fatigue on the road tends to build quietly rather than announce itself.

The biggest change was mental. Instead of stewing on the day I was leaving behind, I started using part of the drive to plan the next one, what needed to happen tomorrow, what I actually wanted to get done. It sounds small. It was not. Treating the drive like a transition instead of dead time made the version of me who walked through the door noticeably calmer than the version who had been walking through it for years.

Week Three, I Started Protecting The Half Hour Before Bed

By week three I had accepted that I could not shorten the commute, so I stopped trying and focused everything on the half hour before bed instead. I cut off caffeine earlier in the day. I moved dinner up whenever I could instead of eating it in front of the television at nine. On the evenings I got home while it was still light out, I used an eye mask so my body was not fighting daylight on top of everything else.

I also started counting my breaths on purpose most nights, four in, hold for a count of seven, out for a count of eight, a few rounds before I even picked up a book. It felt silly the first couple of nights. By the end of the week it had become the signal my body used to actually believe the day was over.

By Week Four, The Watch Mode Finally Turned OFF

I will not pretend every night got fixed. There were still evenings the commute ran ninety minutes and wrecked the whole routine, still nights I caved and scrolled my phone anyway. But the pattern had genuinely shifted. Falling asleep stopped being a fight most nights. I was not lying there running through the drive on a loop anymore. The two in the morning wake ups, staring at the ceiling replaying near misses on the highway, went from a regular thing to something that happens once in a while instead of most nights.

None of this changed my actual commute. The traffic is still the traffic, the trains still stall, the accidents still happen. What changed was everything I did with the time on either side of it. So if you have got a commute you cannot shorten and a job you are not ready to leave, I would ask what you are doing with the drive itself, and with the half hour after it, because that turned out to matter more than the minutes on the clock ever did.

If moving closer to work or finding something local is not on the table right now, a few things helped me without changing my job at all. Swap whatever is raising your heart rate on the drive, news, arguments, stressful calls, for something calmer. Use part of the ride to plan tomorrow instead of replaying today. Protect the same half hour before bed every single night no matter what time you actually got home. And if your commute involves a train or a bus rather than driving, a short nap of ten to thirty minutes can genuinely help, as long as you keep it short enough that it does not eat into your nighttime sleep instead.
